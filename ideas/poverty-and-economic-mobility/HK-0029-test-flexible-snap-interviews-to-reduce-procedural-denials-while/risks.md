## Key risks and failure modes

- A flexible call-in channel could create long queues, abandonment, or new staffing bottlenecks that replace one barrier with another.
- Looser interview timing must not be confused with weaker eligibility verification; poor implementation could increase payment errors or undermine confidence in program integrity.
- Phone-first access may disadvantage applicants without reliable devices, minutes, privacy, stable numbers, hearing access, or language support.
- Identity-verification procedures can become burdensome or exclusionary, especially for applicants with unstable housing or documentation gaps.
- Applicants may disclose sensitive household information in unsafe or non-private settings if privacy protections are weak.
- Aggregate approval gains could conceal unequal completion, error, or wait-time outcomes across disability, language, race/ethnicity, age, family structure, or geography.
- A successful local call-center model may not transfer to states with different staffing systems, caseloads, labor rules, vendor arrangements, or technology.
- Focusing on interview scheduling could distract from other procedural barriers such as verification requests, notices, recertification design, or structural eligibility rules.

## Equity and legitimacy

Any design should preserve non-phone alternatives and accessible accommodations, provide meaningful language access, and measure who reaches the interview channel versus who abandons or is excluded. Applicants and frontline eligibility workers bear the practical burden of the process and should inform usability, privacy, queue design, and fallback pathways before any real-world test. Human reviewers should pay particular attention to people with disabilities, limited English proficiency, unstable housing or contact information, inflexible work schedules, caregiving responsibilities, and limited private phone access. Distributional outcomes should be reported with uncertainty rather than hidden behind average approval effects.
