## Problem

UN peacekeeping practice uses community alert networks, liaison assistants, early warning, and threat assessments to protect civilians. These systems depend on trusted local information and rapid response, but weak handoffs, unclear expectations, unsafe data centralization, and unmeasured response failures can undermine protection.

## Proposed intervention

Design a privacy-preserving early-warning accountability protocol that records only the minimum metadata needed to evaluate whether alerts were received, triaged, acted on, resolved, or missed—without creating a centralized repository of informant identities, tactical details, or sensitive intelligence.

## Theory of change

If community alert systems can measure response reliability and failure modes without exposing sensitive sources, then protection actors may identify broken handoffs, unrealistic response promises, and systematic blind spots while preserving the trust that early warning depends on.

## Falsification condition

Accountability metadata increases retaliation or surveillance risk, communities reduce reporting because they distrust logging, response metrics incentivize unsafe action, or the protocol does not improve detection of missed or delayed protection responses.

## Smallest responsible next step

Study public UN documentation and create a threat model plus minimal metadata schema using synthetic alerts only. Explicitly identify data that must never be centralized. No contact with conflict-affected communities and no operational deployment.
