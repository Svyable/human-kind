## Problem

Extreme heat increasingly threatens health, labor productivity, infrastructure, and urban growth. Many cities have heat plans, warnings, cooling centers, tree programs, and building measures, but portfolios are often evaluated by activities delivered rather than whether vulnerable people actually experience safer temperatures and fewer heat harms.

## Proposed intervention

Develop a city heat-resilience guarantee framework that defines measurable service outcomes—such as maximum response times to dangerous heat, access to safe indoor temperatures, shaded transit/walking routes, worker protections, and neighborhood-level heat exposure reduction—while allowing cities to choose locally appropriate interventions.

## Theory of change

If heat policy is organized around measurable protection outcomes rather than a checklist of projects, then resources can shift toward combinations of warnings, cooling, shade, building retrofits, labor protections, and emergency response that demonstrably reduce exposure for the most vulnerable groups.

## Beneficiaries

- Outdoor and informal workers
- Low-income households without reliable cooling
- Older adults, children, and people vulnerable to extreme heat
- Heat-exposed urban neighborhoods

## Falsification condition

Outcome guarantees prove infeasible to measure or enforce, encourage gaming or displacement, worsen access for vulnerable groups, or perform no better than well-designed conventional heat plans on health, exposure, and equity outcomes.

## Smallest responsible next step

Compare 5-10 existing city heat plans and derive a candidate outcome schema, then test whether the same schema can fairly represent very different climates, housing stocks, and labor conditions. No city outreach or policy change.
