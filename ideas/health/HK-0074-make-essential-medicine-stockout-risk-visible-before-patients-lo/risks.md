## Key risks and failure modes

- False-positive warnings could trigger hoarding, emergency purchasing, unnecessary substitution, or price pressure.
- Publicly exposing granular inventory or supplier data could create security, market-manipulation, or commercial harms.
- Facilities with weaker digital systems could appear safer simply because their data are missing.
- Average national stock can hide local shortages and access inequities.
- Forecasts may overfit routine demand and fail during disasters, epidemics, conflict, or sudden policy changes.
- Substitution recommendations can be clinically inappropriate or create new shortages elsewhere.
- A new data layer could duplicate existing logistics systems and add reporting burden without improving decisions.
- Metrics could reward maintaining inventory rather than reliable patient access, leading to waste or overstocking.

## Equity and legitimacy

The system should treat missing data as uncertainty, not as absence of need. Rural facilities, fragile settings, small buyers, and populations with less administrative visibility must not be systematically deprioritized by a model trained on richer supply chains. Patient-level data are unnecessary for the first validation stage and should not be collected. Any future operational use would require local governance over what supply information can be shared, at what granularity, and with whom.

## People who need a voice before action

- Public-sector pharmacists and medicines-supply managers
- Clinicians who manage shortage substitutions
- Procurement and logistics specialists
- Health-facility representatives from low-resource and fragile settings
- Essential-medicines and health-systems researchers
- Data-governance, security, and competition/market experts
- Patient and chronic-disease advocates before any consequential use
