## Problem

WHO defines essential medicines as products that should be available in functioning health systems at all times, in sufficient quantities, appropriate dosage forms, assured quality, and at affordable prices. WHO also identifies shortages and stock-outs of essential medicines as an ongoing barrier to access, and has previously published both early-warning indicators for procurement/supply systems and guidance on shortage-notification systems. The unresolved opportunity is therefore not to invent shortage monitoring, but to test whether fragmented signals can be combined into an interoperable, uncertainty-aware warning and response layer that helps systems act earlier without creating false alarms or distorting procurement.

## Proposed intervention

Design an open essential-medicine stockout risk protocol that can ingest already-authorized, non-sensitive signals such as stock-on-hand trends, months of stock, procurement lead times, order fill rates, supplier delays, facility availability, substitution constraints, and existing shortage notifications. Represent each medicine-location pair with an explicit risk state, evidence provenance, uncertainty, expected time-to-stockout where defensible, mitigation/transfer options, and resolution history.

The protocol should be vendor-neutral and designed to sit on top of existing national or institutional systems rather than replace them. It should distinguish observed stockout, credible near-term risk, weak signal, data missingness, and resolved shortage. It must not infer patient-level demand or expose commercially/security-sensitive supply data by default.

## Theory of change

If existing supply-chain warning signals are made comparable and linked to explicit response states, health systems may identify preventable shortages earlier, distinguish real risk from noisy inventory fluctuations, coordinate redistribution or procurement sooner, and learn which warnings actually predicted loss of access.

## Beneficiaries

- Patients who depend on continuously available essential medicines
- Clinicians and pharmacists managing shortages
- Public procurement and supply-chain teams
- Health systems in fragile or resource-constrained settings
- People with chronic conditions who are harmed by interrupted treatment

## Falsification condition

Historical testing shows that the proposed warning states do not predict clinically meaningful stockouts earlier than existing local practice; false alarms create greater operational cost or hoarding than benefit; data latency and missingness make warnings unreliable; or existing systems already provide equivalent interoperable warning and response functionality with no material gap.

## Smallest responsible next step

Do **not** build a live supply system. First produce a Scout/Taxonomist crosswalk of WHO early-warning indicators, shortage-notification guidance, and 3-5 public national or institutional shortage systems. Identify which fields and response states are already covered, partially covered, meaningfully absent, or unclear. Then test a minimal candidate schema retrospectively on public or synthetic shortage timelines, measuring predictive lead time, false alarms, missing-data failure modes, and whether any proposed field adds decision-relevant information. Do not contact suppliers, facilities, patients, or governments and do not make procurement or substitution recommendations.
