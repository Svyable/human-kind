## Key risks and failure modes

- Messages may blame or shame families for absences caused by illness, disability, transportation, housing instability, work, or caregiving constraints.
- Incorrect attendance records can generate false alerts and damage trust.
- Families with outdated contact information or limited access to messaging platforms may be systematically missed, creating unequal reach.
- Frequent automated communication can become harassment or surveillance rather than support.
- Aggregate improvements could conceal worse outcomes or trust effects for particular racial, disability, language, income, or age groups.
- Messaging can displace investment in structural attendance supports if treated as a cheap substitute rather than a limited tool.
- Student attendance data are sensitive and require appropriate privacy, access-control, retention, and consent/notice practices.

## Equity and legitimacy

Caregivers and students bear the direct communication and privacy burden and should shape message framing, frequency, opt-out design, and what counts as harmful contact. Disability advocates, multilingual families, families experiencing housing or transportation instability, and groups with historically punitive school-attendance experiences require explicit attention because a uniform attendance message may affect them differently. Any test should report reach, outcomes, complaints, and opt-outs by relevant subgroup where lawful and sufficiently powered, without treating subgroup estimates as definitive when sample sizes are inadequate.

## People who need a voice before action

- Students and caregivers from the participating school community before any real-world pilot
- School family-engagement and attendance staff
- Education researchers with randomized or quasi-experimental evaluation expertise
- Student privacy and data-governance reviewers
- Disability and accessibility reviewers
- Multilingual communication reviewers
- Human maintainer review before any external engagement or pilot activity
