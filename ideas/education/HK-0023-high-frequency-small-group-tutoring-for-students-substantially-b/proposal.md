## Problem

Students who fall substantially behind expected learning goals may not recover through ordinary classroom instruction alone. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized tutoring experiments reported substantial average learning gains but also meaningful heterogeneity across tutor type, grade, setting, dosage, and group size. The relevant question for Human Kind is narrower than “does tutoring work?”: under what conditions can school-day, high-frequency tutoring reliably improve learning for students who are behind without creating unacceptable cost, staffing, stigma, or opportunity-cost burdens?

## Proposed intervention

Provide structured tutoring during the normal school day to students substantially behind local learning goals, typically in one-to-one or very small groups, at high frequency (for example three or more sessions per week). Use a defined curriculum, ongoing formative assessment, trained tutors, and explicit safeguards against replacing core instruction or other valued activities. Treat tutor model, group size, dosage, and technology use as design variables rather than assuming one implementation is universally best.

## Theory of change

If students who are substantially behind receive frequent, targeted instruction in much smaller groups than ordinary classrooms, with rapid feedback and instruction adapted to demonstrated gaps, then their effective practice time and mastery should increase. The mechanism is personalized instructional intensity rather than tutoring as a generic mentoring relationship. Effects should depend on implementation quality, dosage, subject, student age, tutor capacity, and what instructional time tutoring displaces.

## Beneficiaries

- Students substantially behind local grade-level or course learning goals
- Students from lower-income communities where access to private tutoring is limited
- Schools seeking evidence-based learning-recovery options

## Existing work

- https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476 — systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized tutoring experiments; reports substantial average effects and heterogeneity across program characteristics.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w28531 — two randomized trials of intensive math tutoring for Chicago high-school students; reports positive math-test and course-grade effects, with cost and scalability considerations.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32510 — randomized evaluation of a lower-cost tutoring model combining tutor time with computer-assisted learning; reports a 0.23 SD math-test gain and explicitly identifies staffing and cost as scaling constraints.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32039 — randomized trial of short-burst one-to-one early-reading tutoring; provides evidence that tutoring effects can occur under a different subject, age, and delivery model.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32272 — randomized online tutoring evidence from Italy; reports math gains, dosage differences, and smaller effects for group tutoring, broadening the evidence beyond a single U.S. delivery model.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w23792 — randomized high-dosage reading study in New York City with a positive but statistically insignificant mean ELA effect and important subgroup heterogeneity; useful counterevidence against assuming uniform effects.

## Falsification condition

A well-implemented evaluation in the target setting would substantially revise or reject the mechanism if pre-specified learning outcomes do not improve relative to an appropriate comparison condition; if apparent gains are explained by equivalent additional instructional time rather than small-group targeted instruction; if effects disappear outside narrow subgroups or cannot survive realistic staffing and attendance constraints; or if harms such as stigma, displacement of core instruction, reduced attendance, or inequitable access outweigh measured learning gains.
