## Key risks and failure modes

- Tutoring can displace core instruction, electives, rest, or other valued school activities.
- High cost and tutor shortages can make an effective small trial non-scalable or crowd out more cost-effective supports.
- Tutor quality, curriculum alignment, attendance, and dosage may vary enough to erase expected effects.
- Selection into tutoring can stigmatize students or systematically underserve students with disabilities, multilingual learners, or students with unstable attendance.
- Programs may optimize for short-term test performance without durable learning or broader educational benefit.
- Evidence is concentrated in particular countries, grades, subjects, and program models; transfer to other contexts is uncertain.
- Technology-assisted scaling may reduce cost but could change the mechanism or create new access and quality gaps.

## Equity and legitimacy

Eligibility criteria should be transparent and based on demonstrated learning need rather than opaque profiling. Students and families should be informed about what tutoring replaces and how participation affects the school day. Program design needs explicit accommodation for disability, language, transportation, scheduling, and attendance constraints. Distributional effects should be reported by relevant subgroups rather than relying only on an average effect. Affected students, families, educators, and local school communities need a meaningful voice before any implementation decision.

## People who need a voice before action

- Students and families from the target school community
- Teachers, tutors, and school leaders
- Education researchers with randomized-evaluation and implementation expertise
- Special-education and multilingual-learner specialists where relevant
- Local safeguarding, accessibility, and student-support staff
- Budget or operations reviewers able to assess staffing and opportunity costs
