## Sources and comparable work supplied at intake

- https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476 — systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized tutoring experiments; reports substantial average effects and heterogeneity across program characteristics.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w28531 — two randomized trials of intensive math tutoring for Chicago high-school students; reports positive math-test and course-grade effects, with cost and scalability considerations.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32510 — randomized evaluation of a lower-cost tutoring model combining tutor time with computer-assisted learning; reports a 0.23 SD math-test gain and explicitly identifies staffing and cost as scaling constraints.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32039 — randomized trial of short-burst one-to-one early-reading tutoring; provides evidence that tutoring effects can occur under a different subject, age, and delivery model.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w32272 — randomized online tutoring evidence from Italy; reports math gains, dosage differences, and smaller effects for group tutoring, broadening the evidence beyond a single U.S. delivery model.
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w23792 — randomized high-dosage reading study in New York City with a positive but statistically insignificant mean ELA effect and important subgroup heterogeneity; useful counterevidence against assuming uniform effects.

## Central falsifier

A well-implemented evaluation in the target setting would substantially revise or reject the mechanism if pre-specified learning outcomes do not improve relative to an appropriate comparison condition; if apparent gains are explained by equivalent additional instructional time rather than small-group targeted instruction; if effects disappear outside narrow subgroups or cannot survive realistic staffing and attendance constraints; or if harms such as stigma, displacement of core instruction, reduced attendance, or inequitable access outweigh measured learning gains.
